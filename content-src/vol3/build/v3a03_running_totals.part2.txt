## Exercise 1 — year to date

Edit `ex1/solution.sql`.

Works against `orders(id int, customer_id int, status text, total
numeric(10,2), created_at timestamptz)` — 500,000 rows spanning 2022-11-14
to 2023-11-14. Restrict to `created_at >= '2023-01-01'`, which leaves the
eleven months January to November 2023.

Finance wants the 2023 monthly series with year-to-date columns beside it:
how many orders and how much revenue have landed in the year up to and
including each month.

Roll up to one row per month first — `date_trunc('month', created_at)::date`
— and window over those eleven rows. Windowing the half-million raw orders
would produce a running total per *order*, which is a different report.

Illustration on four months:

```
   month    | orders | revenue    | ytd_orders | ytd_revenue
------------+--------+------------+------------+-------------
 2023-01-01 |  42000 | 42100000.00|      42000 | 42100000.00
 2023-02-01 |  38000 | 38200000.00|      80000 | 80300000.00
 2023-03-01 |  41000 | 41300000.00|     121000 | 121600000.00
 2023-04-01 |  40000 | 40050000.00|     161000 | 161650000.00
```

Each `ytd_` column on a row equals the previous row's value plus this row's.
The last row's `ytd_revenue` equals the sum of the whole `revenue` column.

Columns, in this order: `month` (a `date`, the first of the month),
`orders` (that month's order count), `revenue` (that month's revenue sum),
`ytd_orders`, `ytd_revenue`. No rounding.

**Ordered by `month` ascending.** (11 rows.)

```
uv run dojo test v3a03_running_totals ex1
```
@@@FILE ex1/hints.md
## Hint 1

Two stages. The first collapses 500,000 orders into eleven monthly rows; the
second annotates those eleven rows without collapsing them further. Different
tools: one is `GROUP BY`, the other is a window.

## Hint 2

Once the monthly rows exist, both new columns are the same construction:

```sql
sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS ytd_revenue
```

An `ORDER BY` inside `OVER` and no frame clause gives you "everything from
the start of the partition through this row", which is the definition of
year-to-date here.

## Hint 3

`date_trunc('month', created_at)` returns a timestamp; the prompt wants a
`date`, so cast it. The filter `created_at >= '2023-01-01'` belongs inside
the aggregating CTE, where it reduces what gets grouped — not outside it,
where it would have nothing left to do. The decision left to you is whether
to use a CTE or a subquery in `FROM`; they compile to the same plan.
@@@FILE ex1/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Check the arithmetic of the last row before anything else. `ytd_revenue` on
the November row must equal `sum(revenue)` over your entire result. If it is
smaller, your window has a frame you did not intend; if it is larger, your
filter differs between the two stages.

## Hint 2

Here is the version that produces 460,000 rows instead of 11:

```sql
SELECT date_trunc('month', created_at)::date AS month, total,
       sum(total) OVER (ORDER BY created_at) AS ytd_revenue
FROM orders
WHERE created_at >= '2023-01-01';
```

It is a running total, and it is per order. Adding `DISTINCT` to it would
produce something that looks like the answer and is not. The grain of the
report is "one row per month", and the query has to say so with a
`GROUP BY` before any window runs.

## Hint 3

One type note that catches people: `count(*)` is `bigint`, and `sum` of a
`bigint` is `numeric`. So `ytd_orders` comes back as a numeric value rather
than an integer. That is correct and expected — do not cast it back, and do
not be surprised when the column prints without a decimal point but is not
an integer type.
@@@FILE ex1/solution.sql
-- v3a03_running_totals / ex1 — 2023 monthly series with year-to-date columns.
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('month', created_at)::date AS month,
           count(*)   AS orders,
           sum(total) AS revenue
    FROM orders
    WHERE created_at >= '2023-01-01'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT month,
       orders,
       revenue,
       sum(orders)  OVER (ORDER BY month) AS ytd_orders,
       sum(revenue) OVER (ORDER BY month) AS ytd_revenue
FROM monthly
ORDER BY month;
@@@FILE ex1/expected.sql
WITH monthly AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('month', created_at)::date AS month,
           count(*)   AS orders,
           sum(total) AS revenue
    FROM orders
    WHERE created_at >= '2023-01-01'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT m.month,
       m.orders,
       m.revenue,
       (SELECT sum(x.orders)  FROM monthly x WHERE x.month <= m.month) AS ytd_orders,
       (SELECT sum(x.revenue) FROM monthly x WHERE x.month <= m.month) AS ytd_revenue
FROM monthly m
ORDER BY m.month;

## Exercise 2 — an accumulation that resets

Edit `ex2/solution.sql`.

Works against `staff(id int, name text, department text, manager_id int,
salary numeric(10,2), hired_on date)` — 500 rows, hire dates spread over ten
years, five departments.

HR wants a growth curve per department: for each department and each year in
which it hired anybody, how many people it hired that year and how many it
had accumulated by the end of that year. The accumulation must restart at
every department — engineering's first year must not inherit anything from
the department that sorts before it.

Take the year with `extract(year FROM hired_on)::int`.

Illustration across two departments:

```
 department  | hire_year | hires | cumulative_headcount
-------------+-----------+-------+---------------------
 engineering |      2016 |     4 |                    4
 engineering |      2017 |     7 |                   11
 engineering |      2019 |     3 |                   14
 finance     |      2015 |     2 |                    2
 finance     |      2018 |     5 |                    7
```

Two things to read off it. The finance rows start from 2 rather than
continuing engineering's 14 — that is the partition doing its job. And 2018
follows 2015 directly: years in which a department hired nobody produce no
row at all, and for a cumulative count that is harmless.

Columns, in this order: `department`, `hire_year` (an integer), `hires`
(people hired by that department that year), `cumulative_headcount` (the
running total of `hires` within the department, in year order).

**Ordered by `department` ascending, then `hire_year` ascending.**

```
uv run dojo test v3a03_running_totals ex2
```
@@@FILE ex2/hints.md
## Hint 1

The grain of this report is (department, year). Get there with a `GROUP BY`
of two columns, then add one window column on top of the grouped rows.

## Hint 2

The window needs both of its first two clauses:

```sql
sum(n) OVER (PARTITION BY g ORDER BY t)
```

`PARTITION BY` decides where the accumulation resets; `ORDER BY` decides the
direction it accumulates in. Drop either and the column means something
else.

## Hint 3

`extract(year FROM hired_on)` returns a `numeric` in PostgreSQL 14 and
later, so cast it to `int` for a clean output column — and use the same
expression in the `GROUP BY` (or group by the ordinal position). The
decision left to you: group by the expression or by its output alias?
PostgreSQL allows the alias in `GROUP BY`, which is convenient and slightly
non-portable.
@@@FILE ex2/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Predict a property you can check instantly: for each department, the final
`cumulative_headcount` must equal that department's headcount — 138 for
sales, 112 engineering, 98 ops, 91 support, 61 finance. Run your query, look
at the last row of each department, and you will know whether the partition
is right before you read anything else.

## Hint 2

Here is the failure this exercise exists for:

```sql
sum(hires) OVER (ORDER BY department, hire_year)
```

It runs, it climbs, and the last row prints 500. Every department after the
first starts from wherever the previous one ended. The numbers are plausible
enough to survive a glance, which is what makes this bug dangerous rather
than annoying.

## Hint 3

A subtler point, worth thinking about even though it does not change your
answer here: a year in which a department hired nobody is simply absent.
For a *cumulative* count that is fine — the curve is still correct at every
point it reports. It would not be fine for a "hires per year" chart, which
would draw a straight line across the gap, and it would not be fine for a
moving average. That is what exercise 6's calendar spine is for.
@@@FILE ex2/solution.sql
-- v3a03_running_totals / ex2 — departmental headcount curves, resetting per group.
WITH hires AS (
    SELECT department,
           extract(year FROM hired_on)::int AS hire_year,
           count(*)                         AS hires
    FROM staff
    GROUP BY 1, 2
)
SELECT department,
       hire_year,
       hires,
       sum(hires) OVER (PARTITION BY department
                        ORDER BY hire_year) AS cumulative_headcount
FROM hires
ORDER BY department, hire_year;
@@@FILE ex2/expected.sql
WITH hires AS (
    SELECT department,
           extract(year FROM hired_on)::int AS hire_year,
           count(*)                         AS hires
    FROM staff
    GROUP BY 1, 2
)
SELECT h.department,
       h.hire_year,
       h.hires,
       (SELECT sum(x.hires)
          FROM hires x
         WHERE x.department = h.department
           AND x.hire_year <= h.hire_year) AS cumulative_headcount
FROM hires h
ORDER BY h.department, h.hire_year;

## Exercise 3 — high-water marks

Edit `ex3/solution.sql`.

Works against `events(id bigint, customer_id int, event_type text, payload
jsonb, ts timestamptz)` — 3,000,000 rows, one per second, `ts` running from
2023-10-11 to 2023-11-14. Restrict to `event_type = 'purchase'`.

Growth wants the daily purchase series with three cumulative columns, all
from the same window: the total so far, the best single day seen so far, and
how many days of data have been reported so far.

Roll up to one row per day with `date_trunc('day', ts)::date`.

Illustration on five days:

```
    day     | purchases | purchases_so_far | best_day_so_far | days_so_far
------------+-----------+------------------+-----------------+------------
 2023-10-11 |      4100 |             4100 |            4100 |           1
 2023-10-12 |      6600 |            10700 |            6600 |           2
 2023-10-13 |      5200 |            15900 |            6600 |           3
 2023-10-14 |      6900 |            22800 |            6900 |           4
 2023-10-15 |      3300 |            26100 |            6900 |           5
```

`best_day_so_far` never goes down — that is what makes it a high-water mark,
and it is also the cheapest way to check your own answer. If your column
dips anywhere, the window is wrong.

Columns, in this order: `day` (a `date`), `purchases` (that day's purchase
count), `purchases_so_far`, `best_day_so_far`, `days_so_far`.

**Ordered by `day` ascending.**

```
uv run dojo test v3a03_running_totals ex3
```
@@@FILE ex3/hints.md
## Hint 1

Three cumulative columns, one window. The only thing that changes between
them is which aggregate you wrap around the frame.

## Hint 2

Every aggregate becomes cumulative over an ordered window:

```sql
sum(x)   OVER (ORDER BY d)   -- total so far
min(x)   OVER (ORDER BY d)   -- worst so far
count(*) OVER (ORDER BY d)   -- rows so far
bool_or(flag) OVER (ORDER BY d) -- has it happened yet
```

Nothing about the frame changes; the meaning comes entirely from the
function.

## Hint 3

Declare the window once with a `WINDOW` clause and use it three times — it
makes the query shorter and makes it structurally impossible for the three
frames to drift apart. The decision left to you: `count(*)` or
`count(purchases)`? On this rollup there are no NULLs, so they agree; say
which one you mean anyway.
@@@FILE ex3/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

The `events` table has three million rows and no index on `event_type`, so
this query will do a sequential scan. That is fine and expected — but it
means you want exactly one scan. Aggregate to days inside a CTE, and let the
windows run over the thirty-odd rows that come out. If you find yourself
windowing over raw events, stop.

## Hint 2

Verify with an invariant rather than by eye. Three of them are available:
`purchases_so_far` on the last row equals `sum(purchases)` over the whole
result; `best_day_so_far` on the last row equals `max(purchases)`;
`days_so_far` on the last row equals the number of rows. Check all three and
the query is proved.

## Hint 3

One thing to notice about `best_day_so_far`: because the frame only grows,
PostgreSQL cannot compute this one incrementally the way it does a sum —
there is no way to un-maximise a value when a row leaves the frame. Here the
frame never shrinks, so it does not matter. It starts to matter with a
sliding `max` over a wide frame, and knowing that now will save you a
mystifying slow query later.
@@@FILE ex3/solution.sql
-- v3a03_running_totals / ex3 — daily purchases with three cumulative columns.
WITH daily AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('day', ts)::date AS day,
           count(*)                    AS purchases
    FROM events
    WHERE event_type = 'purchase'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT day,
       purchases,
       sum(purchases) OVER w AS purchases_so_far,
       max(purchases) OVER w AS best_day_so_far,
       count(*)       OVER w AS days_so_far
FROM daily
WINDOW w AS (ORDER BY day)
ORDER BY day;
@@@FILE ex3/expected.sql
WITH daily AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('day', ts)::date AS day,
           count(*)                    AS purchases
    FROM events
    WHERE event_type = 'purchase'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT d.day,
       d.purchases,
       (SELECT sum(x.purchases) FROM daily x WHERE x.day <= d.day) AS purchases_so_far,
       (SELECT max(x.purchases) FROM daily x WHERE x.day <= d.day) AS best_day_so_far,
       (SELECT count(*)         FROM daily x WHERE x.day <= d.day) AS days_so_far
FROM daily d
ORDER BY d.day;

## Exercise 4 — a trailing average, honestly

Edit `ex4/solution.sql`.

Works against `orders(id int, customer_id int, status text, total
numeric(10,2), created_at timestamptz)`, October 2023 only
(`created_at >= '2023-10-01' AND created_at < '2023-11-01'`), rolled up to
one row per day. October has orders on all 31 days.

Analytics wants a seven-day trailing average of daily revenue — and they
want to stop publishing the first six values, which are averages of one to
six days wearing a seven-day label.

Three columns do that: the naive average, the number of rows the frame
actually contained, and the honest average that is NULL until the frame is
full.

Illustration on the first eight days:

```
    day     | revenue  | ma7_naive | days_in_window | ma7
------------+----------+-----------+----------------+----------
 2023-10-01 | 41500.00 |  41500.00 |              1 |
 2023-10-02 | 38200.00 |  39850.00 |              2 |
 2023-10-03 | 44100.00 |  41266.67 |              3 |
 2023-10-04 | 39900.00 |  40925.00 |              4 |
 2023-10-05 | 42800.00 |  41300.00 |              5 |
 2023-10-06 | 40100.00 |  41100.00 |              6 |
 2023-10-07 | 44900.00 |  41642.86 |              7 | 41642.86
 2023-10-08 | 39500.00 |  41357.14 |              7 | 41357.14
```

`ma7` is empty until the seventh row and equals `ma7_naive` from then on.
Note that `days_in_window` stops at 7 and stays there — the frame slides, it
does not keep growing.

Columns, in this order: `day` (a `date`), `revenue`, `ma7_naive` (the mean
over the current day and the six before it, rounded to 2 decimals),
`days_in_window` (how many rows that frame held), `ma7` (the same average,
but NULL unless `days_in_window` is exactly 7).

**Ordered by `day` ascending.** (31 rows.)

```
uv run dojo test v3a03_running_totals ex4
```
@@@FILE ex4/hints.md
## Hint 1

All three derived columns look at the same seven rows. Write that frame
once, name it, and use it three times.

## Hint 2

A frame with both bounds specified, and a count over it:

```sql
avg(x)   OVER (ORDER BY d ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
count(*) OVER (ORDER BY d ROWS BETWEEN 2 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
```

The `count` is how you find out whether the `avg` had three rows to work
with or fewer.

## Hint 3

For the third column, a `CASE` may wrap a window function — that is legal
and ordinary, because `CASE` is evaluated in the select list, after
windowing. What is *not* legal is filtering on a window function in `WHERE`.
With no `ELSE`, a `CASE` yields NULL, which is exactly the value the prompt
wants for the first six rows.
@@@FILE ex4/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Count the rows in the frame by hand for the first three days. Day 1: the
frame is "six before me and me", but there is nothing before me, so it holds
one row. Day 2: two rows. Day 7: seven. Day 8: still seven, because the
oldest day has slid out of the back. A frame that specifies both bounds
slides; a frame with `UNBOUNDED PRECEDING` grows forever. Know which one you
have written.

## Hint 2

The temptation is to filter the partial rows away:

```sql
... WHERE count(*) OVER w = 7
```

PostgreSQL rejects it — window functions cannot appear in `WHERE`. The
options are a `CASE` in the select list (which keeps all 31 rows, as the
prompt wants) or a second query level with the filter outside (which would
give you 25 rows, which the prompt does not want). Read the required row
count before choosing.

## Hint 3

`avg` over a `numeric` column returns `numeric`, so `round(..., 2)` applies
directly. If you find yourself needing `::numeric`, something upstream has
become a float — check that you did not divide by an integer literal
somewhere, or use `extract(epoch ...)` on a timestamp.
@@@FILE ex4/solution.sql
-- v3a03_running_totals / ex4 — a seven-day trailing mean that admits its edges.
WITH daily AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('day', created_at)::date AS day,
           sum(total)                          AS revenue
    FROM orders
    WHERE created_at >= '2023-10-01'
      AND created_at <  '2023-11-01'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT day,
       revenue,
       round(avg(revenue) OVER w, 2) AS ma7_naive,
       count(*) OVER w               AS days_in_window,
       CASE WHEN count(*) OVER w = 7
            THEN round(avg(revenue) OVER w, 2)
       END                           AS ma7
FROM daily
WINDOW w AS (ORDER BY day ROWS BETWEEN 6 PRECEDING AND CURRENT ROW)
ORDER BY day;
@@@FILE ex4/expected.sql
WITH daily AS (
    SELECT date_trunc('day', created_at)::date AS day,
           sum(total)                          AS revenue
    FROM orders
    WHERE created_at >= '2023-10-01'
      AND created_at <  '2023-11-01'
    GROUP BY 1
)
SELECT d.day,
       d.revenue,
       (SELECT round(avg(x.revenue), 2)
          FROM daily x
         WHERE x.day BETWEEN d.day - 6 AND d.day) AS ma7_naive,
       (SELECT count(*)
          FROM daily x
         WHERE x.day BETWEEN d.day - 6 AND d.day) AS days_in_window,
       CASE WHEN (SELECT count(*)
                    FROM daily x
                   WHERE x.day BETWEEN d.day - 6 AND d.day) = 7
            THEN (SELECT round(avg(x.revenue), 2)
                    FROM daily x
                   WHERE x.day BETWEEN d.day - 6 AND d.day)
       END                                        AS ma7
FROM daily d
ORDER BY d.day;
