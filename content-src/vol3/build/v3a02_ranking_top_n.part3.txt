## Exercise 5 — WITH TIES

Edit `ex5/solution.sql`.

Works against `staff(id int, name text, department text, manager_id int,
salary numeric(10,2), hired_on date)`, restricted to the 61 people in
`finance`.

Compensation wants "the top five, plus anyone tied with the fifth". They are
comparing salary *bands*, not exact salaries: band each person with
`round(salary, -4)` — a negative scale, so the salary rounds to the nearest
ten thousand — and take the top five rows by band, keeping every row that
ties with the fifth.

Use `FETCH FIRST 5 ROWS WITH TIES` rather than a ranking function. It is
standard SQL, PostgreSQL has had it since version 13, and it works on the
whole result — which is exactly the situation here.

Illustration with bands holding 3, 4 and 2 people:

```
 id | salary    | band
----+-----------+-------
  9 | 121000.00 | 120000
  3 | 118000.00 | 120000
 17 | 124000.00 | 120000
  4 | 112000.00 | 110000
 22 | 108000.00 | 110000
 31 | 107000.00 | 110000
  8 | 113000.00 | 110000
```

Seven rows for a "top five": the fifth row sits in band 110000, so the other
two members of that band come with it. The band below is excluded entirely.

One trap to plan for. `WITH TIES` decides what counts as a tie using the
**entire** `ORDER BY`, so adding `, id` to it would make every row unique
and silently turn the clause into a plain `LIMIT 5`. The required output
ordering therefore cannot live in the same `ORDER BY` — wrap the limited
query and sort outside it.

## Exercise 6 — NULLs in the ordering

Edit `ex6/solution.sql`.

Works against `staff(id int, name text, department text, manager_id int,
salary numeric(10,2), hired_on date)`, joined to itself: a person's *direct
reports* are the rows whose `manager_id` equals that person's `id`. Restrict

HR wants finance staff ranked by how well paid their direct reports are.
Most of them manage nobody, so their average report salary is NULL — and
NULL is not zero, it is "no such number".

The point of the exercise is what a ranking does with that NULL. In
PostgreSQL, `ORDER BY x DESC` puts NULLs **first** by default, so under a
naive ranking every leaf employee ties for first place, above everyone who
actually manages people. `NULLS LAST` moves them to the bottom, where a
report about managers wants them.

Illustration on six finance staff:

```
 id | reports_avg | rank_nulls_last | rank_default
----+-------------+-----------------+-------------
  2 |             |               5 |            1
  7 |             |               5 |            1
 11 |             |               5 |            1
  4 |    98000.00 |               1 |            4
  9 |    91000.00 |               2 |            5
 13 |    77500.00 |               3 |            6
```

Three NULL rows tie in both columns — all NULLs are peers of each other.
Under `rank_nulls_last` they share rank 5, one past the three real managers;
under `rank_default` they share rank 1 and push the real managers down to 4.

Columns, in this order: `id`, `name`, `reports_avg` (the mean salary of this
person's direct reports, rounded to 2 decimals, NULL when they have none),

WITH managers AS (
    SELECT m.id,
           m.name,
           round(avg(r.salary), 2) AS reports_avg
    FROM staff m
    LEFT JOIN staff r ON r.manager_id = m.id
    WHERE m.department = 'finance'
    GROUP BY m.id, m.name
),

    SELECT count(*) AS n FROM managers WHERE reports_avg IS NULL
)
SELECT g.id,
       g.name,
       g.reports_avg,
       CASE WHEN g.reports_avg IS NULL
            THEN (SELECT count(*) + 1 FROM managers x WHERE x.reports_avg IS NOT NULL)
            ELSE (SELECT count(*) + 1 FROM managers x WHERE x.reports_avg > g.reports_avg)
       END AS rank_nulls_last,
       CASE WHEN g.reports_avg IS NULL
            THEN 1::bigint
            ELSE n.n + 1 + (SELECT count(*) FROM managers y WHERE y.reports_avg > g.reports_avg)
       END AS rank_default
FROM managers g

ORDER BY g.id;

## Hint 1

## Hint 2

The most common `LATERAL` failure is forgetting the keyword:

@@@FILE review1/hints.md
## Hint 1

Read the requirement one more time: "the next distinct band down is band 2".
Distinct values, numbered consecutively. That sentence names a function.

## Hint 2

The distinguishing test, on scores 10, 10, 9:

```
row_number -> 1, 2, 3
rank       -> 1, 1, 3
dense_rank -> 1, 1, 2
```

Only one of them gives the third row a 2.

## Hint 3

The window orders by the band expression, descending. You may write
`round(salary, -4)` twice — once in the select list as `band`, once in the
window's `ORDER BY` — or order by the output alias; PostgreSQL accepts the
alias in a statement-level `ORDER BY` but **not** inside an `OVER` clause,
so the window needs the expression itself. That is the decision this
exercise leaves you.
@@@FILE review1/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Check your answer's shape before its values. `max(band_rank)` in your result
must equal the number of distinct bands in finance — nothing more. If your
maximum is close to 61, you numbered people; if it skips values, you counted
people ahead of each row rather than bands.

## Hint 2

A different route to the same answer, useful as a cross-check: collect the
distinct bands into a CTE, count how many are strictly greater than this
row's band, add one. That is the definition of `dense_rank` written out
longhand, and running both should give you identical columns.

## Hint 3

One thing worth noticing on the way out: `dense_rank` over a *banded*
expression is doing something you could also do with arithmetic —
`(max_band - band) / 10000 + 1` — and the two disagree the moment a band is
empty. `dense_rank` numbers the bands that exist; the arithmetic numbers the
bands that could exist. Which one is right depends on the question, and
knowing that they differ is the review.
@@@FILE review1/solution.sql
-- v3a02_ranking_top_n / review1 — which salary band, counting from the top.
SELECT id,
       name,
       salary,
       round(salary, -4) AS band,
       dense_rank() OVER (ORDER BY round(salary, -4) DESC) AS band_rank
FROM staff
WHERE department = 'finance'
ORDER BY band DESC, id;
@@@FILE review1/expected.sql
WITH fin AS (
    SELECT id, name, salary, round(salary, -4) AS band
    FROM staff
    WHERE department = 'finance'
),
bands AS (
    SELECT DISTINCT band FROM fin
)
SELECT f.id,
       f.name,
       f.salary,
       f.band,
       (SELECT count(*) + 1 FROM bands b WHERE b.band > f.band) AS band_rank
FROM fin f
ORDER BY f.band DESC, f.id;
