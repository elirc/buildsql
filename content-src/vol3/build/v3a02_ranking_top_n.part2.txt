## Exercise 1 — the three functions, side by side

Edit `ex1/solution.sql`.

Works against `customers(id int, name text, email text, country text,
created_at timestamptz)` and `orders(id int, customer_id int, status text,
total numeric(10,2), created_at timestamptz)`. Restrict to the twenty
customers with `id` from 1 to 20 — small enough to read the whole result.

Support wants a leaderboard of those twenty by how many orders they have
placed, numbered three different ways so they can decide which numbering
they actually want. Customers who have never ordered must still appear, with
an order count of `0`.

Illustration on six customers:

```
 id | order_count | rn | rnk | drnk
----+-------------+----+-----+-----
  4 |          12 |  1 |   1 |    1
  1 |           9 |  2 |   2 |    2
  7 |           9 |  3 |   2 |    2
  2 |           5 |  4 |   4 |    3
  9 |           5 |  5 |   4 |    3
  3 |           0 |  6 |   6 |    4
```

Read the tie at 9: `rn` numbers the two rows 2 and 3 (and needs `id` in its
window's `ORDER BY` to decide which is which), `rnk` gives both 2 and then
skips to 4, `drnk` gives both 2 and continues at 3.

Columns, in this order: `id`, `name`, `order_count`, `rn`
(`row_number`, ordered by `order_count` descending then `id` ascending),
`rnk` (`rank`, ordered by `order_count` descending only), `drnk`
(`dense_rank`, ordered by `order_count` descending only).

**Ordered by `order_count` descending, then `id` ascending.** (20 rows.)

```
uv run dojo test v3a02_ranking_top_n ex1
```
@@@FILE ex1/hints.md
## Hint 1

Two stages. First you need one row per customer carrying a count — that is
an aggregate over a join. Then you need three numberings of those rows —
that is three window functions. Windows are computed after `GROUP BY`, so
both stages can live in one query if you want them to.

## Hint 2

A window function may take an aggregate as its ordering expression in a
grouped query:

```sql
SELECT department, count(*) AS headcount,
       rank() OVER (ORDER BY count(*) DESC) AS size_rank
FROM staff
GROUP BY department;
```

The window sees the five post-aggregation rows, not the 500 input rows.

## Hint 3

The customer with no orders is the row that decides whether your join is
right. `LEFT JOIN orders` keeps them; `count(o.id)` counts non-NULL order
ids and gives `0`, while `count(*)` would count the single NULL-padded row
and give `1`. The decision left to you is whether to do it in one query or
to build the counts in a CTE first — both are correct, and one of them is
much easier to debug.
@@@FILE ex1/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Predict the three columns before you write them. Take the scores 12, 9, 9,
5, 5, 0 and write out `row_number`, `rank` and `dense_rank` by hand. If your
`rank` column ever prints 3, you have written `dense_rank`; if your
`dense_rank` ever skips a number, you have written `rank`.

## Hint 2

Now consider the failure that produces a plausible-looking wrong answer:

```sql
row_number() OVER (ORDER BY count(o.id) DESC)
```

Two customers tie on nine orders. Nothing here decides which gets 2 and
which gets 3 — and PostgreSQL is free to answer differently on a different
plan. The prompt specifies the tie-break for exactly this reason. Note that
`rnk` and `drnk` do *not* need it: they give tied rows the same number by
definition.

## Hint 3

If your zero-order customer is missing entirely, your join is inner. If they
appear with `1`, you counted rows instead of order ids. Both bugs put them
in the wrong place in the ranking, which is why the prompt's illustration
puts a zero at the bottom.
@@@FILE ex1/solution.sql
-- v3a02_ranking_top_n / ex1 — row_number, rank and dense_rank on one result.
SELECT c.id,
       c.name,
       count(o.id) AS order_count,
       row_number() OVER (ORDER BY count(o.id) DESC, c.id) AS rn,
       rank()       OVER (ORDER BY count(o.id) DESC)       AS rnk,
       dense_rank() OVER (ORDER BY count(o.id) DESC)       AS drnk
FROM customers c
LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
WHERE c.id BETWEEN 1 AND 20
GROUP BY c.id, c.name
ORDER BY order_count DESC, c.id;
@@@FILE ex1/expected.sql
WITH counts AS (
    SELECT c.id,
           c.name,
           count(o.id) AS order_count
    FROM customers c
    LEFT JOIN orders o ON o.customer_id = c.id
    WHERE c.id BETWEEN 1 AND 20
    GROUP BY c.id, c.name
)
SELECT id,
       name,
       order_count,
       row_number() OVER (ORDER BY order_count DESC, id) AS rn,
       rank()       OVER (ORDER BY order_count DESC)     AS rnk,
       dense_rank() OVER (ORDER BY order_count DESC)     AS drnk
FROM counts
ORDER BY order_count DESC, id;

## Exercise 2 — ranking inside partitions

Edit `ex2/solution.sql`.

Works against `staff(id int, name text, department text, manager_id int,
salary numeric(10,2), hired_on date)` — all 500 rows. Department sizes are
sales 138, engineering 112, ops 98, support 91, finance 61.

HR wants an internal pay-position sheet: where each person sits within their
own department, best paid first, and how many people they are being compared
against. Ties share a position — two people on identical salaries are both
"joint 4th" — so this is `rank`, not `row_number`.

Illustration across two small departments:

```
 id | department  | salary    | dept_rank | dept_size
----+-------------+-----------+-----------+----------
  9 | finance     | 120000.00 |         1 |         3
  3 | finance     |  90000.00 |         2 |         3
  7 | finance     |  80000.00 |         3 |         3
  2 | ops         | 150000.00 |         1 |         4
  5 | ops         | 110000.00 |         2 |         4
 11 | ops         | 110000.00 |         2 |         4
  1 | ops         |  70000.00 |         4 |         4
```

Two things to notice: the numbering restarts at 1 for each department, and
after the shared 2 the next value is 4, not 3.

Columns, in this order: `id`, `name`, `department`, `salary`, `dept_rank`
(rank within the department by salary descending, ties shared), `dept_size`
(headcount of that department).

**Ordered by `department` ascending, then `dept_rank` ascending, then `id`
ascending.** (500 rows.)

```
uv run dojo test v3a02_ranking_top_n ex2
```
@@@FILE ex2/hints.md
## Hint 1

Both new columns are computed over "the rows that share this person's
department". One of them also cares about the order within that group; the
other does not.

## Hint 2

The two windows differ by one clause:

```sql
rank()   OVER (PARTITION BY category ORDER BY price DESC) -- position in category
count(*) OVER (PARTITION BY category)                     -- size of category
```

Adding an `ORDER BY` to the second one would turn a headcount into a
running count — which is a different report.

## Hint 3

`rank()` never needs a tie-break in its window, because tied rows are meant
to share a number. Your statement-level `ORDER BY` does need one, though:
without `, id` at the end, the two people sharing rank 2 come back in an
arbitrary order. That is the decision this exercise leaves you: the window's
ordering and the output's ordering are different clauses solving different
problems.
@@@FILE ex2/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Sanity-check the shape before the values. The query must return 500 rows —
one per person, not one per department. If yours returns 5, you reached for
`GROUP BY`; grouping collapses, and this report needs every individual row
annotated.

## Hint 2

Diagnose this variant, which runs and is wrong:

```sql
rank() OVER (ORDER BY salary DESC)
```

The 138th-best-paid person in sales gets a company-wide number in the
hundreds. Every department except the top one looks broken. One clause is
missing, and it is the first of the three decisions in an `OVER`.

## Hint 3

Now the subtler variant. Suppose you write `dense_rank()` instead of
`rank()`. The output still looks sensible — numbers ascending, restarting
per department — but after a tie it continues at 3 rather than 4, so two
people on identical salaries no longer "use up" a position. The prompt's
illustration says which behaviour is wanted; read the row after the tie.
@@@FILE ex2/solution.sql
-- v3a02_ranking_top_n / ex2 — pay position within each department.
SELECT id,
       name,
       department,
       salary,
       rank()   OVER (PARTITION BY department ORDER BY salary DESC) AS dept_rank,
       count(*) OVER (PARTITION BY department)                      AS dept_size
FROM staff
ORDER BY department, dept_rank, id;
@@@FILE ex2/expected.sql
SELECT s.id,
       s.name,
       s.department,
       s.salary,
       (SELECT count(*) + 1
          FROM staff x
         WHERE x.department = s.department
           AND x.salary > s.salary) AS dept_rank,
       (SELECT count(*)
          FROM staff y
         WHERE y.department = s.department) AS dept_size
FROM staff s
ORDER BY s.department, dept_rank, s.id;

## Exercise 3 — the canonical top-N

Edit `ex3/solution.sql`.

Works against `staff(id int, name text, department text, manager_id int,
salary numeric(10,2), hired_on date)` — 500 rows, five departments.

The board wants the three highest-paid people in each department: exactly
three per department, fifteen rows in total, no more and no fewer. Ties are
broken by the lower `id` winning, and that tie-break must be inside the
window so the answer is reproducible.

Illustration for one department:

```
 department | dept_rank | id | name        | salary
------------+-----------+----+-------------+-----------
 finance    |         1 | 12 | Ana Duarte  | 190000.00
 finance    |         2 |  4 | Rob Chen    | 175000.00
 finance    |         3 | 31 | Mia Okafor  | 175000.00
```

Rows 2 and 3 have identical salaries; `id` 4 beats `id` 31, so it takes
second place. A fourth person also on 175000.00 would **not** appear —
exactly three rows per department is the requirement.

Note that you cannot filter on a window function in the same `SELECT` that
computes it. `WHERE dept_rank <= 3` alongside the `row_number()` call is a
syntax error; the ranking has to happen one query level down.

Columns, in this order: `department`, `dept_rank`, `id`, `name`, `salary`.

**Ordered by `department` ascending, then `dept_rank` ascending.**
(15 rows.)

```
uv run dojo test v3a02_ranking_top_n ex3
```
@@@FILE ex3/hints.md
## Hint 1

You need a number that does not exist yet at the time `WHERE` runs. So build
the numbered rows first, in their own query level, and filter the result of
that.

## Hint 2

The two-level shape, on a different table:

```sql
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT category, id, name, price,
           row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY category
                              ORDER BY price DESC, id) AS rn
    FROM products
)
SELECT category, rn, id, name, price
FROM ranked
WHERE rn <= 2
ORDER BY category, rn;
```

A CTE and a subquery are interchangeable here; pick whichever you find
readable.

## Hint 3

"Exactly three" is the specification that chooses your function.
`row_number` guarantees three; `rank` would keep everybody tied for third
and can return four or more. The decision left to you is the tie-break
column — put it in the window's `ORDER BY`, not only in the final one, or
the *membership* of the top three (not just its display order) becomes
arbitrary.
@@@FILE ex3/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Start by counting rows. Five departments, three each: 15. If your result has
3 rows, you forgot `PARTITION BY` and ranked the whole company. If it has
more than 15, you used `rank` or `dense_rank` and ties are leaking in. The
row count diagnoses the bug before you read a single name.

## Hint 2

Try the illegal version deliberately:

```sql
SELECT department, id, name, salary,
       row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY department ORDER BY salary DESC, id) AS dept_rank
FROM staff
WHERE row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY department ORDER BY salary DESC, id) <= 3;
```

PostgreSQL answers: `window functions are not allowed in WHERE`. The reason
is evaluation order — `WHERE` runs before windowing, so the value does not
exist yet. Every top-N query in the world is two levels deep because of that
sentence.

## Hint 3

There is a second correct shape worth knowing, and it is not a window at
all: a `LATERAL` subquery per department, each with its own `ORDER BY
salary DESC, id LIMIT 3`. It returns the same fifteen rows. Exercise 7 makes
you write it; for now, notice that "top N per group" has two idioms and you
are learning the general one first.
@@@FILE ex3/solution.sql
-- v3a02_ranking_top_n / ex3 — exactly three highest paid per department.
WITH ranked AS (
    SELECT department,
           id,
           name,
           salary,
           row_number() OVER (PARTITION BY department
                              ORDER BY salary DESC, id) AS dept_rank
    FROM staff
)
SELECT department,
       dept_rank,
       id,
       name,
       salary
FROM ranked
WHERE dept_rank <= 3
ORDER BY department, dept_rank;
@@@FILE ex3/expected.sql
SELECT d.department,
       p.dept_rank,
       p.id,
       p.name,
       p.salary
FROM (SELECT DISTINCT department FROM staff) d
CROSS JOIN LATERAL (
    SELECT s.id,
           s.name,
           s.salary,
           row_number() OVER (ORDER BY s.salary DESC, s.id) AS dept_rank
    FROM staff s
    WHERE s.department = d.department
    ORDER BY s.salary DESC, s.id
    LIMIT 3
) p
ORDER BY d.department, p.dept_rank;

## Exercise 4 — keeping the ties

Edit `ex4/solution.sql`.

Works against `reviews(id int, product_id int, rating int, body text,
created_at timestamptz)` — 1,000,000 rows over 10,000 products, so roughly a
hundred reviews each. Restrict to `product_id <= 100`.

Merchandising wants the most-reviewed products in that range — "the top
three, and if anything ties for third, keep it too". Review counts are small
integers over a hundred products, so ties are not hypothetical: expect the
result to contain more than three rows.

Illustration where two products tie for second and three tie for third:

```
 product_id | review_count | rnk
------------+--------------+-----
         17 |          128 |   1
          4 |          119 |   2
         62 |          119 |   2
          9 |          115 |   4
         41 |          115 |   4
         88 |          115 |   4
```

Six rows for a "top three" — because rank 2 is shared by two products and
rank 4 by three, and the cut is on the *rank value*, not the row count. Note
that rank 3 does not exist: `rank` skips after a tie.

Columns, in this order: `product_id`, `review_count`, `rnk` (the rank by
review count descending, ties shared). Keep every row whose `rnk` is 3 or
less.

**Ordered by `rnk` ascending, then `product_id` ascending.**

```
uv run dojo test v3a02_ranking_top_n ex4
```
@@@FILE ex4/hints.md
## Hint 1

Three stages, and it helps to name them: count the reviews per product,
rank the counts, keep the small ranks. Each stage is a query level.

## Hint 2

The filter is on the rank, so the ranking must already exist:

```sql
WITH counts AS (
    SELECT category, count(*) AS n FROM products GROUP BY category
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT category, n, rank() OVER (ORDER BY n DESC) AS rnk FROM counts
)
SELECT * FROM ranked WHERE rnk <= 2;
```

Chained CTEs read top to bottom; each may refer to the ones above it.

## Hint 3

The choice of ranking function is the exercise. `row_number() <= 3` would
return exactly three rows and silently discard products tied with the third.
The prompt wants them kept, which selects one specific function — and note
that with that function your result's row count is data-dependent, so do not
assert three rows anywhere.
@@@FILE ex4/importantHints.md
## Hint 1

Imagine the requirement being handed to you verbally: "give me the top
three". Ask the follow-up out loud — "and if four products tie for third?"
The three possible answers map exactly onto `row_number`, `rank` and
`dense_rank`. This prompt has answered it for you; find the sentence that
does.

## Hint 2

Watch what `dense_rank() <= 3` would do here instead. It keeps the top three
distinct *counts*, which on this data could be a dozen products, because
many products share counts. It is not wrong — it is the answer to "the top
three levels", a question nobody asked. Predict the difference before you
run either.

## Hint 3

One practical note on scale: the filter `product_id <= 100` belongs inside
the counting stage, not outside the ranking one. Put it in the outer query
and you rank all ten thousand products and then throw away 99% of them —
same answer, a hundred times the work. Predicates that can run before the
window should.
@@@FILE ex4/solution.sql
-- v3a02_ranking_top_n / ex4 — top three review counts, ties kept.
WITH counts AS (
    SELECT product_id,
           count(*) AS review_count
    FROM reviews
    WHERE product_id <= 100
    GROUP BY product_id
),
ranked AS (
    SELECT product_id,
           review_count,
           rank() OVER (ORDER BY review_count DESC) AS rnk
    FROM counts
)
SELECT product_id,
       review_count,
       rnk
FROM ranked
WHERE rnk <= 3
ORDER BY rnk, product_id;
@@@FILE ex4/expected.sql
WITH counts AS (
    SELECT product_id,
           count(*) AS review_count
    FROM reviews
    WHERE product_id <= 100
    GROUP BY product_id
)
SELECT c.product_id,
       c.review_count,
       (SELECT count(*) + 1
          FROM counts x
         WHERE x.review_count > c.review_count) AS rnk
FROM counts c
WHERE (SELECT count(*)
         FROM counts y
        WHERE y.review_count > c.review_count) < 3
ORDER BY rnk, c.product_id;
